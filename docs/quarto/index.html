Encephalopathy and In-Hospital Outcomes Among Adults Hospitalized With Gastrointestinal Bleeding: A National Inpatient Sample Analysis, 2017–2022

NIS Analysis for Research Council of Pakistan

Author
Affiliation

Muhammad Umer Sohail

Dow University of Health Sciences

Preamble

  • Reference Papers:

    • Goble SR, Ismail AS, Debes JD, Leventhal TM. Critical care outcomes in decompensated cirrhosis: a United States national inpatient sample cross-sectional study. Crit Care. 2024;28:150.
    • Rudler M, de Matharel M, Bouzbib C, Mouri S, Kheloufi L, Weiss N, Sultanik P, Thabut D. Multiple concomitant precipitating factors of hepatic encephalopathy are associated with a poor prognosis in patients with cirrhosis admitted to intensive care unit. United European Gastroenterology Journal. 2025;13(5):738–749.
    • Farooq U, Tarar ZI, Franco D, Kamal F, Abegunde AT. Comparison of outcomes between variceal and non-variceal gastrointestinal bleeding in patients with cirrhosis: insights from a Nationwide Inpatient Sample. Annals of Gastroenterology. 2022;35(6):618–626.

  • Study Objective: Estimate the association between encephalopathy coded as a secondary diagnosis and in-hospital mortality among adults hospitalized with gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding, and compare other in-hospital outcomes and resource use.

  • Data Source: Cross-sectional analysis of the National Inpatient Sample (NIS) from 2017 to 2022, all six years pooled.

  • Patient Selection: Included adult (age 18 years or older) admissions with GI bleeding as the principal diagnosis (variceal, nonvariceal upper, lower or unspecified bleeding, defined by bleeding-specific ICD-10-CM principal-diagnosis codes). Transfers to another short-term hospital were kept. Admissions with missing mortality, sex or primary payer were excluded. The exposure was encephalopathy coded in a secondary diagnosis position (diagnosis fields 2 to 40): G93.40, G93.41 and G93.49, any G92 code, I67.4 (hypertensive encephalopathy) and E51.2 (Wernicke encephalopathy).

  • Outcomes:

    • In-hospital mortality (primary)
    • Invasive mechanical ventilation
    • Acute kidney injury, shock, aspiration pneumonia and respiratory failure (secondary diagnoses)
    • Red blood cell transfusion
    • Non-home discharge among survivors with a known destination (transfer to another short-term hospital, transfer to a skilled nursing, intermediate care or other facility, or discharge against medical advice, compared with routine or home health discharge)
    • Length of stay (days) and inflation-adjusted hospital cost, standardized to 2022 USD
    • Upper endoscopy (inspection code 0DJ08ZZ), endoscopic control of bleeding (0W3P8ZZ) and lower endoscopy (inspection code 0DJD8ZZ) (descriptive only)

  • Covariates:

    • Demographics: age, sex, race, median household income quartile by patient ZIP code, and primary payer.
    • Hospital characteristics: hospital region, bed size, and location/teaching status.
    • Comorbidity: Elixhauser comorbidity count (Quan ICD-10 algorithm, excluding the other neurological disorders category, which contains G93.4, and the liver disease and alcohol abuse categories, which overlap the cirrhosis, portal hypertension and F10 covariates; non-cirrhotic liver disease is therefore not separately adjusted for), cirrhosis, portal hypertension, alcohol-related disorder (F10) and COVID-19 (U07.1) in any diagnosis position.
    • Bleeding source (principal diagnosis): variceal, nonvariceal upper, lower, or unspecified.
    • Calendar year, entered as a categorical factor.

  • Statistical Analysis:

    • All analyses incorporated NIS discharge weights (DISCWT), strata and clustering. Hospital and stratum codes were made year-specific before pooling 2017–2022.
    • Ratios (percentages, means, medians, odds ratios, ratios of means) use the discharge weights unchanged.
    • Mortality was modeled with a survey-weighted logistic regression (quasibinomial family), reported as an adjusted odds ratio. The adjusted average risk difference was also estimated from predicted margins: each admission was predicted with encephalopathy set to present and to absent, and the survey-weighted mean difference was taken over the complete-case estimation sample; its 95% CI uses a delta-method standard error from the model covariance, conditional on the sample covariate distribution.
    • The primary mortality model adjusts for calendar year, age, sex, race, income quartile, payer, hospital region, bed size, location/teaching status, Elixhauser count, cirrhosis, portal hypertension, alcohol-related disorder, bleeding source and COVID-19.
    • Binary outcome models (including subgroup models) use the same covariates. For any binary model with fewer than 10 events per model parameter (intercept excluded), a reduced model adjusting only for age, sex, calendar year and Elixhauser count is used; the covariates shown in each table identify the model fitted.
    • Length of stay was modeled with a survey-weighted quasi-Poisson log-link model, and cost with a survey-weighted Gamma log-link model restricted to positive costs, both reported as ratios of means.
    • Subgroups: mortality was estimated separately in admissions with and without cirrhosis, and in variceal and all other GI bleeding admissions. The encephalopathy-by-cirrhosis and encephalopathy-by-variceal-bleeding interactions were each tested with a survey Wald test; for the variceal interaction, bleeding source is entered as variceal vs. other in this model.
    • Models use complete cases for race, income quartile, bed size, location/teaching status, hospital region and Elixhauser count.

  • Limitations:

    • Present-on-admission status is not available in the NIS, so secondary diagnoses cannot be separated from complications that developed after admission, and a present-on-admission sensitivity analysis is not feasible. All secondary-diagnosis results are associations.
    • Encephalopathy coding depends on clinical documentation and coding intensity, which may differ between hospitals and years. Coding intensity bias is possible.
    • Transfers and admissions with unknown discharge destination limit the interpretation of non-home discharge and of outcomes after transfer.

  • Software: All analyses were performed using R Version 4.6.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).
